Part 1 — What is Cognitive Computing?

Imagine you're running a business with thousands of customer transactions hitting your desk every day. Your experienced analysts observe what's happening, apply their knowledge, make judgment calls, and decide what to do. The business runs on human cognition — the mental process of perceiving, learning, and reasoning.

👁️

Perception

Gathering raw information from the world before anything useful can happen.

→
🧠

Learning

Recognizing patterns from repeated exposure to data over time.

→
⚖️

Reasoning

Weighing evidence and arriving at a defensible conclusion.

Perception is the foundation — gathering raw information. If this step is broken, nothing downstream can fix it. Learning is accumulated pattern recognition built from repeated exposure. A senior analyst's sharp instincts aren't magic — they're thousands of data points internalized over years. Reasoning is where information becomes a decision: weighing what was perceived and learned to arrive at a defensible conclusion.

Cognitive computing replicates this three-part process. Unlike traditional tools that simply execute commands, cognitive systems are designed to grasp user needs, anticipate questions, and proactively deliver insights. They don't just do what you tell them — they think alongside you.

Part 2 — Discovery: The Real-World Workflow

Before building anything, we need to understand what the people who will use this technology actually do every day. This is discovery work — and it's one of the most important instincts a product manager can have. Below is the current-state process flow for commercial loan underwriting.

Part 2b — Underwriting Sub-Process

This is the step where cognitive computing has the greatest potential impact. Below is the internal workflow an underwriter follows — the nine steps where perception, learning, and reasoning all converge.

The underwriter organizes all documents into a single reviewable package, then pulls key financial data points: revenue, expenses, cash flow, debt, credit scores, collateral values.

If key data is missing, the process loops back to collect what's needed before analysis can continue.

The underwriter assesses financial health (cash flow vs. debt service, performance trajectory) and evaluates broader risk factors: industry stability, market conditions, management track record.

The analysis is translated into a standardized risk rating, documented in a written assessment, and submitted to the credit committee for final decision.

Part 3 — The Pen and Paper Test

Now that we've mapped the workflow, we need to ask: why is cognitive computing specifically a good fit? Strip away the technology and explain why the core concepts apply using plain language.

Perception — Is the Right Information Being Collected?

The underwriter sits down with financial statements, tax returns, bank statements, credit reports — all unstructured, arriving in different formats and at different levels of completeness.

The pen and paper check: If the underwriter is working with an incomplete package, no amount of analysis downstream will fix that. Clean, complete data at intake is the foundation.

Learning — Do We Have Enough History to Learn From?

A senior underwriter who has reviewed thousands of deals has developed pattern recognition that isn't in any policy manual. They recognize financial signatures that signal strength or trouble.

The pen and paper check: If the historical data is thin, biased, or poorly recorded, the learning will produce bad instincts. Past confidence is not the same as past accuracy. Historical decisions must be validated against actual loan outcomes.

Reasoning — Do We Know What a Good Decision Looks Like?

The underwriter synthesizes everything into a defensible decision. A borrower with a high debt ratio but strong cash flow in a stable industry might be a better risk than a borrower with clean numbers in a volatile sector.

The pen and paper check: If ten underwriters aren't sharing consistent criteria for what constitutes an approvable borrower, even perfect data and perfect pattern recognition will produce inconsistent outcomes.

Three Questions Before Building Anything

  1. Are we collecting the right information? Is intake complete? Have we identified all data sources?
  2. Do we have a solid, honest track record to learn from? Has it been validated against actual outcomes?
  3. Do we know what a good decision looks like? Are criteria consistent and institutionally agreed upon?
