Part 1

What is Cognitive Computing?

Cognitive computing replicates the three-part mental process that experienced professionals use every day — and builds technology that thinks alongside them, not just for them.

Video walkthrough — The Concept
Coming soon
👁️

Perception

Gathering raw information from the world. If this step is broken — incomplete data, wrong sources — nothing downstream can fix it.

🧠

Learning

Recognizing patterns from repeated exposure. A senior analyst's instincts aren't magic — they're thousands of data points internalized over years.

⚖️

Reasoning

Weighing evidence to arrive at a defensible conclusion. This is where information becomes a decision.

The key difference: Unlike traditional tools that execute commands, cognitive systems are designed to grasp context, anticipate questions, and proactively surface insights. They think alongside you.

Part 3

The Pen and Paper Test

Now that the workflow is mapped, the question becomes: does cognitive computing actually apply here? Three checks, in plain language.

Video walkthrough — Why It Fits
Coming soon
👁️ Perception

The underwriter works with financial statements, tax returns, credit reports — all unstructured, arriving in different formats at different levels of completeness.

⚠ If the package is incomplete, no amount of analysis downstream will fix it.

🧠 Learning

Senior underwriters have reviewed thousands of deals and developed pattern recognition that isn't in any policy manual — financial signatures that signal strength or trouble.

⚠ If the historical data is thin or biased, the learning will produce bad instincts. Past confidence ≠ past accuracy.

⚖️ Reasoning

The underwriter synthesizes everything into a defensible decision. A high debt ratio with strong cash flow in a stable industry can be a better risk than clean numbers in a volatile sector.

⚠ If ten underwriters aren't sharing consistent criteria, even perfect data produces inconsistent outcomes.

Three Questions Before Building Anything

  1. Are we collecting the right information? Is intake complete and sourced correctly?
  2. Do we have a solid track record to learn from? Validated against actual outcomes?
  3. Do we know what a good decision looks like? Criteria consistent and institutionally agreed?
