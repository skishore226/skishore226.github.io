Cognitive Computing

From concept to working prototype — this project walks through how a product manager would discover, document, and build an AI-augmented underwriting tool grounded in cognitive computing principles.

Part 1

What is Cognitive Computing?

Imagine you're running a business with thousands of customer transactions hitting your desk every day. You have a team of experienced analysts and managers. They observe what's happening, apply their knowledge, make judgment calls, and decide what to do. The business runs on human cognition — the mental process of perceiving, learning, and reasoning. Let's break that down.

Perception

When an analyst reads a document, listens to a customer, or reviews a transaction slip, they're doing the most fundamental part of the job: gathering raw information from the world around them.

But here is the key: Nothing useful happens if this step is broken. For instance, if the analyst is only reading half the forms, or missing verbal customer complaints, no amount of smart analysis later will fix that. It all starts with information intake.

Learning

Now hand that analyst's notes to someone senior — someone who has reviewed thousands of transactions over the years. Over time, they've started recognizing patterns: "this type of transaction always precedes fraud," or "customers who ask this question usually need X." They might not be able to articulate every rule they follow, but their instincts are sharp. That expertise isn't magic — it's accumulated pattern recognition built from repeated exposure to data.

Reasoning

The senior analyst then brings their findings to a manager, who weighs the evidence and makes a call: approve the transaction, flag it for review, escalate the complaint, or some other decision. This is reasoning — taking what was perceived and learned and arriving at a defensible conclusion. It's where information becomes a decision.

So what is Cognitive Computing?

This three-part process — perceive, learn, reason — is exactly what cognitive computing replicates. Cognitive computing is a branch of AI that mimics these human thought processes to create intelligent machines. But unlike traditional tools that simply execute commands, cognitive systems are designed to grasp user needs, anticipate questions, and proactively deliver insights. They don't just do what you tell them — they think alongside you.

Part 2

A Real-World Workflow: Commercial Loan Underwriting

Why start with the workflow?

Before we build anything, engineer anything, or design anything, we need to understand what the people who will use this technology actually do every day. This is discovery work — and it's one of the most important instincts a product manager can have.

In the real world, this means sitting down with the end users inside a bank — loan officers, underwriters, credit committee members — and mapping out their process step by step. Not how the system works. How they work.

This matters because one of the most common failures in digital transformation projects — whether the technology is a CRM, a cloud platform, or an AI tool — is that the technology gets built without being properly integrated into real-world workflows. Teams ship features that look great in a demo but break the way people actually do their jobs. The result is software that gets worked around instead of worked with.

So before we talk about cognitive computing as a solution, let's do the discovery. Below is the current-state process flow for commercial loan underwriting — the kind of artifact a product manager would produce after spending time with the people who live this process every day.

Step-by-Step Walkthrough

1

Borrower Inquiry & Initial Conversation

A commercial customer reaches out to the lending institution to finance a capital project. A relationship manager has a high-level qualifying conversation to understand how much they're looking to borrow, the purpose, and the general financial profile of the business. It's essentially lead qualification — the RM determines whether there's enough fit to move forward.

2

Application Intake & Document Collection

The borrower decides to proceed, and the bank sends a loan application with a checklist of required documents: financial statements, tax returns, bank statements, business plans, proof of collateral, guarantees, and more. In practice, this phase takes at least two to three weeks — the data is unstructured and arrives in pieces. The RM tracks what's been received, follows up on what's missing, and confirms the package is complete enough to move forward.

3

Preliminary Review & Deal Structuring

A loan officer or credit analyst checks whether the deal aligns with the bank's lending criteria — industry, borrower type, geography, loan size. Many institutions have restrictions that apply case by case, so a borrower with decent credit might still be declined because of a policy restriction. If the deal falls outside policy, it's declined early. If it fits, the loan officer begins structuring terms: loan amount, interest rate, repayment schedule, and collateral requirements.

4

Underwriting (Subprocess)

This is the most cognitively intensive step. The underwriter takes the full document package and conducts a detailed analysis: financial health, cash flow, debt, credit history, plus broader context like the loan's purpose, the industry, and market conditions. They synthesize all of it into a written assessment with a risk rating and recommendation. The underwriter can only recommend — they cannot approve. We'll break this subprocess down in detail below.

5

Credit Committee Review & Decision

The underwriter's assessment goes to a senior credit officer or formal committee. Whether it's a single approver or a full committee depends on the deal size. The committee reviews the analysis, asks questions, and can approve, send it back for restructuring, or decline.

6

Loan Approval & Commitment Letter

Once approved, the institution sends a commitment letter to the borrower outlining final terms and conditions. The borrower reviews it, may negotiate, and signs.

7

Closing & Documentation

Legal documents are drafted — loan agreements, personal guarantees, UCC filings. Both parties execute the documents, funds are disbursed, and the loan is booked onto the institution's portfolio.

8

Portfolio Management

After funding, the institution monitors the borrower's performance over the life of the loan: periodic financial reviews, compliance checks against covenants, collateral monitoring, and risk re-rating if the borrower's situation changes. A borrower who was healthy at closing might later show signs of distress, requiring restructuring or workout.

The underwriter analyzes whether the borrower can repay the loan: cash flow vs. debt service, performance trajectory, consistency across documents. This requires interpreting data in context, not just reading it.

Beyond the financials: industry stability, market conditions, loan purpose, management track record. The underwriter weighs variables that interact differently from deal to deal.

The analysis is translated into a standardized risk rating. The rating must be defensible — traceable to evidence in the loan file, not a gut feeling.

The completed assessment and risk rating are submitted for review and final decision — the handoff back into step 5 of the main workflow.

Part 3

The Pen and Paper Test: Why Cognitive Computing Fits Underwriting

Now that we've mapped the workflow, we need to ask: why is cognitive computing specifically a good fit for underwriting? To answer that, we apply what I call the pen and paper test — strip away the technology entirely and explain why the core concepts apply using plain language.

The Core Problem

Imagine a mid-market lending institution with hundreds of loan applications coming through the door, each accompanied by a pile of documents. A team of experienced underwriters reviews all this information, applies their knowledge, makes judgment calls, and arrives at decisions. The institution runs on their cognition.

But this doesn't scale. Underwriters are slow, expensive, and inconsistent at volume. When loan volume increases, quality and turnaround time slip. Applications sit in queues, borrowers go to competitors, and junior analysts brought on to help don't have the experience to make consistent decisions. The question becomes: can we replicate that underwriter's cognitive process — perception, learning, and reasoning — at scale?

Perception — Is the Right Information Being Collected?

Perception in underwriting is the act of reviewing the loan package. The underwriter sits down with financial statements, tax returns, bank statements, credit reports, business plans, collateral documentation — all unstructured, arriving in different formats, at different levels of completeness.

The pen and paper check: If the underwriter is working with an incomplete package — missing collateral data, gaps in credit history, incomplete financials — no amount of analysis downstream will fix that. There may also be alternative data sources that could strengthen the analysis but haven't been identified. Clean, complete data at intake is the foundation.

Learning — Do We Have Enough Historical Data to Learn From?

A senior underwriter who has reviewed thousands of deals has developed pattern recognition that isn't in any policy manual. They recognize financial signatures that signal strength or trouble — signals that become visible only when you've seen enough deals.

The pen and paper check: A junior underwriter won't spot what a veteran sees — and the same is true for any system. If the historical data is thin, biased, or poorly recorded, the learning will produce bad instincts. Past confidence is not the same as past accuracy. Historical decisions must be validated against actual loan outcomes. Much of this expertise lives in people's heads and has never been captured in structured form.

Reasoning — Do We Know What a Good Decision Looks Like?

Reasoning is where the underwriter makes the call — synthesizing everything into a defensible decision. A borrower with a high debt ratio but strong cash flow in a stable industry might be a better risk than a borrower with clean numbers in a volatile sector with no management track record.

The pen and paper check: If ten underwriters aren't sharing consistent criteria for what constitutes an approvable borrower, even perfect data and perfect pattern recognition will produce inconsistent outcomes. The reasoning process is only as good as the logic behind it.

Three Questions Before Building Anything

  1. Are we collecting the right information? Is the loan package intake process complete? Have we identified all data sources, including alternative sources and institutional knowledge trapped in people's heads?
  2. Do we have a solid, honest track record to learn from? Is the historical data reliable? Has it been validated against actual loan performance?
  3. Do we know what a good decision looks like? Are the decision criteria well-defined, consistent across underwriters, and agreed upon institutionally?
Part 4

The Working Prototype

To validate the analysis above, I built a working AI-powered prototype called the Meridian Commercial Bank Underwriting Workbench. The application uses real-time AI analysis (not mocked data) to walk each loan through the three stages of cognitive computing, with the underwriter holding final decision authority at every step.

Below is a walkthrough of each stage in action.

Stage 1: Perception — Document Intake & Extraction

The system reads the full loan package, extracts key financial data points into a structured table with source attribution, and runs a completeness check. If required documents are missing, analysis is blocked until the gap is resolved.
